# Grading Scale Explained: A–F to Percentages

*By Muhammad Salman*

Every school seems to run its own version of the same idea: turn a percentage into a letter, and turn that letter into a number for GPA. This guide walks through the standard **grading scale** used across most US classrooms, what each letter actually signals, and where schools quietly diverge from it — with the site's own [grading scale calculator](/grading-scale/) and [grade chart](/grade-chart/) linked throughout if you'd rather skip the math than do it by hand.

## Grading Scale Explained: A–F to Percentages

### The Standard A–F Grading Scale (Percentage Breakdown)

Most US schools run on the same basic **letter grading system**, even when the exact **cutoffs** differ from classroom to classroom: a **100-point scale** gets converted into a small set of letters that act as a **numeric equivalent** for how a student performed on a test or in a class. Every teacher's **syllabus** should spell out where those lines fall, but the table below is the version used almost everywhere as the default.

| Letter | Percentage | GPA Points | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| A+ | 97–100% | 4.0 | Exceptional |
| A | 93–96% | 4.0 | Excellent |
| A- | 90–92% | 3.7 | Near excellent |
| B+ | 87–89% | 3.3 | Above average |
| B | 83–86% | 3.0 | Good |
| B- | 80–82% | 2.7 | Slightly above average |
| C+ | 77–79% | 2.3 | Decent |
| C | 73–76% | 2.0 | Average |
| C- | 70–72% | 1.7 | Below average |
| D+ | 67–69% | 1.3 | Struggling |
| D | 63–66% | 1.0 | Poor |
| D- | 60–62% | 0.7 | Barely passing |
| F | Below 60% | 0.0 | Failing |

Historically the scale ran **A through D** plus F, skipping **E** entirely — a holdover from **historical European conventions** that never fully caught on in US schools, since a grade meant to signal **course failure** looked too much like it stood for something else. Colleges add their own twist on top of this: a strong **GPA point value** across your transcript feeds directly into **scholarships** and **graduate applications**, which is exactly why the site's own grading scale calculator converts a raw score straight into both the letter and the number in one step, instead of making you look up a table by hand.

### What Each Letter Grade Means

**Honor roll** lists and **merit scholarships** are usually reserved for students posting an A, since that grade signals **strong comprehension** and real command of the material rather than a lucky guess — it's also the baseline most **competitive college programs** expect to see repeated across a transcript, and it's the number that drives **class rank** and **weighted GPA calculations** at schools offering AP or Honors sections. A B still reads as **good performance** with **above-average understanding**, giving a **solid foundation** and **competitive standing** at most **four-year universities**, while a C is genuinely **average performance** — a **passing grade** that keeps **cumulative GPA** intact but starts to limit which **prerequisites** and **graduate school** programs stay realistically open.

A D is **technically passing** but reflects **below-average performance**: plenty of **core courses** — especially in **nursing** or **engineering** — require a C or better, so a D can quietly **disqualify continuation** in a specific major even though the transcript still shows an **unsatisfactory passing grade** rather than a fail. An F means **failing** outright, showing **sufficient material understanding** was never reached, and unlike a D it usually can't be patched with extra credit — it requires **retaking for credit**, and only a school's own **grade forgiveness** or **repeat policies** determine whether the new grade replaces the old one on that **semester**'s record.

### Plus and Minus Grades Explained

Not every school bothers with **minus modifiers** on top of the **main letter grades** — some report a flat A/B/C/D/F and nothing else — but where **plus modifiers** are used, each letter splits into up to **three variations**, giving a much finer read on where a student actually landed. F is the one exception: there's **no F+** and **no F-**, since a fail doesn't get graded on a curve of its own.

The gap between a B+ and a B- works out to **0.6 GPA points**, which sounds small until it's multiplied across a full **semester GPA** — six classes at the low end of each letter instead of the high end can shift a report card by nearly **half a point**, and that gap compounds over **four years** into a genuinely different GPA range. Schools don't even agree on where the **thresholds** sit for the **highest performance level**: an A+ might require 97% at one school and a stricter **96.5** or **98** at another, so the exact **cutoff thresholds** are always worth checking against a specific syllabus rather than assumed.

### Why Is There No E Grade?

The letter grading system is often traced back to a school in **Massachusetts** — **Mount Holyoke College**, around **1897** — where the original scale actually included an E for a failing mark. The commonly cited origin story is that both **parents** and **teachers** kept **misread** E as standing for "Excellent," which defeated the entire point of the letter.

**F for Fail** solved the confusion because it's completely **unambiguous**, and by the **early 1900s** most **American schools** had standardized on A through D plus F, with the E **dropped permanently** from the mainstream scale. A handful of programs still use it in narrow contexts today, but for the vast majority of US classrooms, F has been the failing grade for well over a century.

### How the Grading Scale Connects to GPA

A **Grade Point Average** is just the letter-grade table above converted into a single number, typically running from **0.00** up to **4.00**, and it's the figure that actually gets checked against **graduation requirements**: **undergraduate students** generally need at least a **2.0 GPA** to graduate, while **graduate students** chasing **scholarship recipients** status — or keeping one at all — are usually held to a **3.0 GPA** floor.

The **GPA calculation** itself weights every grade by its **course credits** — a **4-credit lab course** with a heavy **workload** pulls harder on the final number than a light **1-credit seminar** does — which is where **credit hours** and **quality points** come in. A **practical example**: **five 3-credit courses** graded A, A, B+, B, and C add up to **48.9** quality points over **15 credits**, for a **3.26 GPA** — noticeably lower than the **3.575** it would've been without that one C, exactly the kind of **GPA management** trade-off that makes **high-credit courses** worth extra attention before a big test.

### What Counts as a Passing Grade?

The honest answer depends on **location** and **objectives** — a passing grade at most **US high schools** simply means clearing F and earning **credit** toward graduation, but **college** raises the bar considerably. Many **major-specific courses**, especially in **Pre-Med** or **Engineering**, quietly require **C or better** rather than treating a bare pass as good enough, since a D signals **inadequate foundational knowledge** for building on in the next course.

That leaves **D grades** sitting in genuinely **ambiguous territory**: **technical passage** through **university programs**, but often labeled an **unsatisfactory passing** result that doesn't actually count toward **academic advancement**. It matters for real stakes too — **financial aid** and a **minimum GPA** for **good standing** can hinge on avoiding too many D's, falling into **academic probation** hits **graduate school applications** hard, and **international students** in particular should confirm with their **academic advisors** whether a D-range grade will even **transfer** for **degree credit**, since many receiving institutions and **pass/fail courses** won't accept it. A **65** specifically tends to land right in this D range on most standard scales — technically passing, but rarely comfortable.

### How Grading Scales Vary by School and Class Type

A student earning **91%** might land an A at one school and a flat B+ at the next, because the **A threshold** genuinely moves between institutions — always check the actual **course syllabus** rather than assuming the standard table applies. **AP** and **Honors courses** frequently run on a **weighted GPA** instead, where an A is worth **5.0** rather than 4.0, and a few schools go further still with **4.33 scales** that hand out **small GPA bonuses** for an A+; competitive fields like **medicine** and **law** often apply **stricter cutoffs** on top of whichever base scale a program uses. The site's grade chart is built to handle both the standard and weighted versions side by side, so switching between a normal class and an AP one doesn't mean redoing the math by hand.

There's a **hidden workload** side to this too that rarely makes it into the grading-scale conversation: a **credit hour** is nominally one hour of **weekly classroom instruction**, but **professional guidelines** put the real time commitment at roughly triple that once outside work is counted — a **four-credit History course** or a **two-credit lab** each carry study hours well beyond their listed credit number, working out to something like **twelve weekly hours** for a single class. Add that up across **full-time enrollment** and **15 credits per semester** becomes closer to **45 weekly hours** total, the actual pace needed to reach the **120 total credits** most four-year degrees require on schedule.

### Beyond A–F: Standards-Based and Weighted Grading

**SBG** — short for **Standards-Based Grading**, sometimes called **Mastery** grading or **Competency-Based Grading** — skips the **traditional 0-100 point system** entirely in favor of a **four-category scale** describing **proficiency level** directly: labels like **Exceeds Standard**, **Meets Standard**, and **Approaching Standard** report whether **demonstrated competency** was actually reached, rather than compressing it into a single letter. A handful of **liberal arts institutions** go a step further with **narrative evaluations** — full **written professor evaluations** instead of any **mastery scale** number at all.

Schools that do want to convert an SBG result back into something that fits a normal GPA generally lean on the **percentage method** over more complex options like the **mean**, **mode**, or **decaying average** calculations — or a named framework like the **Marzano method** — mainly because it's the easiest one to explain **consistency** on across an entire school, even if it loses some of the nuance those other methods were built to capture.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a 70 a passing grade?**
At most US high schools, yes — a 70% sits in the C- range on the standard scale, above both the D and F thresholds. In college the same 70% is technically passing too, but some programs set a higher bar (C or better) for it to actually count toward a major.

**What grade is an 85 percent on the standard grading scale?**
An 85% falls squarely in the B range (83–86%), worth 3.0 GPA points on most 4.0 scales. It's above the B- cutoff at 82% and below the B+ range that starts at 87%.

**Is a C+ considered a good grade?**
A C+ (77–79%, 2.3 GPA points) is above a straight C but still below B-, so it reads as solid rather than strong. For a difficult elective outside your main focus it's usually fine; for a prerequisite in a competitive major, it's worth pushing higher.

**What is the lowest passing grade on a typical grading scale?**
On most standard scales, D- (60–62%) is the lowest grade that still counts as passing — anything below 60% becomes an F. That said, "passing" and "good enough for your program" aren't the same thing, since many majors require C or better regardless.

**Does a D hurt your GPA?**
Yes, noticeably. A D is only worth 1.0 GPA point, so even one D mixed into a semester of B's and A's pulls the average down more than most students expect, and it can also fail to satisfy course-specific requirements even while technically passing.

**What is a grading scale calculator and how does it work?**
A grading scale calculator takes the points a student earned against the total points possible, converts that into a percentage, and then maps the percentage onto the letter-grade table automatically. Instead of looking up a cutoff table by hand, you get the letter and GPA points in one step — that's exactly what the tool linked at the top of this page does.

**What is the difference between a grading scale and a grade chart?**
A grading scale is the rule set — which percentage ranges map to which letters and GPA points. A grade chart is usually the visual, printable version of that same rule set, handy for a classroom wall or a quick glance without opening a calculator.

**Is a B+ worth more GPA points than a B?**
Yes — on a standard 4.0 scale, B+ is worth 3.3 GPA points versus 3.0 for a flat B, a 0.3-point difference. It's a small gap on a single course but adds up across a full transcript.

**Do all schools and teachers use the same grading scale?**
No. The percentage breakdown in this article is the most common version in the US, but individual schools and even individual teachers set their own cutoffs — some put the A threshold at 90%, others at 93% or higher. Always check the actual syllabus for a specific class.

**What is weighted grading, and how is it different from a normal grading scale?**
Weighted grading adds extra GPA points for harder classes — typically AP or Honors courses — so an A might be worth 5.0 instead of the usual 4.0. It uses the same letter-grade table underneath; it just changes how many GPA points each letter is worth once class difficulty is factored in.

**How do I convert my percentage grade to a letter grade quickly?**
Match your percentage against the standard table: 90% and up is generally an A, 80–89% a B, 70–79% a C, 60–69% a D, and below 60% an F, with plus/minus versions splitting each range further at most schools.
